# Part 4 — The counterfactual: what would have had to be true for LABOUR/ILO to work?

The LABOUR/ILO strategy was not merely "spend more". It was a conditional
bet, and the conditions were parameters. Four had to hold jointly.

**1. A large accelerator, and a demand impulse big enough to trigger it.**
The strategy required private investment to respond strongly to demand
rather than to fiscal signals. Nattrass herself notes Chirinko's (1993:1883)
finding that lagged demand is the single most significant empirical
determinant of investment, so this was the better-supported prior even in
1996. My Part 3 estimates are consistent with it: excluding the 2009 and
2020 recessions, a one-point rise in lagged GDP growth is associated with a
0.75–0.94 point rise in real private investment growth (p < 0.01), while the
deficit coefficient loses significance entirely. On this parameter the
subsequent evidence broadly supports LABOUR/ILO — though the result is
fragile to GDP vintage, and no coefficient here is causal.

**2. Employment elasticity high enough to convert growth into jobs.**
Redistribution required demand-led growth to be labour-absorbing. The
2001–2006 evidence is more favourable than the standard "jobless growth"
story allows: formal non-agricultural employment grew 1.9–4.5% a year
through that expansion, briefly beating GEAR's own 4.3% target in 2006.
Demand-led growth did create formal jobs when it arrived. But Nattrass's
warning cuts against reading too much into this: rising average labour
productivity — pursued deliberately under "high productivity now" industrial
and wage policy — mechanically lowers the employment elasticity of output.
The strategy needed labour-demanding growth while its own allies were
bargaining for the wage floors that discourage it. Actual real wage growth
in 1996–99 (1.7, 2.3, 8.6, 3.0%) massively exceeded GEAR's assumed path
(−0.5, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0%), and formal employment fell every one of those years.
That is the internal contradiction Nattrass identified, and it is unresolved.

**3. No punitive capital-account response.** This is where Nattrass
criticises LABOUR/ILO most sharply, and where the counterfactual is weakest.
The strategy assumed policy autonomy that an open capital account does not
grant. A deficit-financed expansion only works if the risk premium and
capital outflow response to fiscal divergence are small. South Africa's
subsequent history says they are not: the 2001 currency collapse, the 2008
reversal, and the post-2020 debt trajectory all show how quickly financing
conditions tighten. Nattrass's contrast with the East Asian economies is
decisive here — Malaysia and Thailand could run large deficits because they
had mobilised deep domestic savings pools and were therefore "less prone to
destabilising capital flight". South Africa's savings rate was, and remains,
low. The same deficit is simply a different instrument in a low-savings,
open-capital-account economy.

**4. A demand expansion that does not destabilise.** Nattrass is explicit
that the demand–investment relationship has a limit: if expansion undermines
macroeconomic balance through inflation and depreciation, profitability is
threatened and investment falls rather than rises. Latin American
macroeconomic populism is her counter-example. So LABOUR/ILO needed to be
right not only that demand drives investment, but that it could deliver
demand *without* triggering the instability that destroys it — a narrow path.

**Verdict.** The counterfactual is not "LABOUR/ILO would have worked". It is
that LABOUR/ILO was right about the mechanism GEAR got wrong — investment
follows demand, not fiscal signalling — while being wrong, or at least
unserious, about the constraint GEAR took seriously. Nattrass's criticism of
both camps survives my own evidence intact: GEAR bet on a confidence channel
my regressions cannot find, and LABOUR/ILO bet on policy space that
globalisation and a low savings rate did not provide.
